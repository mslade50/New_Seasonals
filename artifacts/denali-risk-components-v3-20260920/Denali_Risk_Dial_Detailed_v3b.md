# Denali Risk Dial Team Introduction

The goal of the risk dial is to proactively identify good and bad trading environments—the “easy dollar” versus “hard penny” markets many seasoned traders recognize. It looks beneath index price action for signs of risk that a steady or rising market can hide.

The dial combines the seven components below into one score. Higher readings reflect stronger combined warnings. We focus on what happened over the next **5, 10 and 21 trading days**—roughly one week, two weeks and one month.

SPY is an exchange-traded fund that tracks the S&P 500. Each component table compares returns following dates when its warning was active with returns across all dates in the study. This revision updates the 17 September introduction for one model change and one research result.

## Distribution Dominance

Counts distribution and accumulation days over the past 63 trading sessions. Both require SPY volume above its 63-session average and more than 15% above the previous session. Distribution days close below the open; accumulation days close above it. A large selling imbalance near index highs can reveal demand weakening beneath a strong index.

## NYSE Net Highs

Subtracts new 52-week lows from new 52-week highs on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE). A negative total means more listed securities are making lows than highs. When SPY is still near its own high, that mismatch suggests the index is masking weakness across the broader market. Since 18 September the warning reads a five-session average of that total rather than a single day’s figure, so one quiet day inside a negative stretch no longer switches it on or off.

| Average SPY return | Next 5 | Next 10 | Next 21 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Warning active (73 dates) | -0.14% | -0.20% | +0.87% |
| All dates (1,790) | +0.33% | +0.65% | +1.38% |

The NYSE warning carries full strength when SPY is less than 2% below its trailing 252-session closing high, and 60% strength from 2% through 3%. The five-session average reaching zero or above clears it. Otherwise, its influence fades with time and falling prices.

**Why the five-session average.** Across the longer breadth history it flags 244 days in 34 separate warnings, where the single-day version flagged 345 days in 78, and after a flag SPY averaged -0.41%, -0.63% and -0.80% over the next 5, 10 and 21 days against +0.19%, +0.38% and +0.76% on comparable near-high days whose breadth was not negative. The chance of a 5% decline within the following quarter was 67% rather than 52%. That is why the row above now sits below the all-date comparison at 5 and 10 days, where it used to sit above it. The caveat is that measured from each version’s first warning both still show positive 21-day returns, and the averaged version arrives about four days later, so against a placebo that simply waits those days the improvement is not statistically clear. We made the change to stop the warning flickering—in August it cleared and rebuilt three times in eleven sessions—not because it forecasts better.

## What the ranges mean

**Below 20 — supportive:** the strongest average returns across all three windows. **20–40 — constructive:** positive returns, with smaller gains than the under-20 group. **40–50 — caution:** gains narrowed further. **50+ — warning:** taken together this group's averages were negative across all three windows, with the deterioration concentrated above 60. **80+ — rare high readings:** the weakest sample averages, based on only 25 dates.

## Why 50 matters

Fifty is our working warning threshold. It separates a group with positive average returns across all three windows from one with negative averages. The individual bands show a gradual deterioration. Fifty gives us a practical dividing line. The score gives us a consistent way to recognize when trading conditions have become less forgiving.

The components describe different kinds of pressure: selling activity, defensive leadership, volatility, sector behavior, seasonality and market breadth. Their weights, how recently they fired and the market backdrop determine the score. Reading the component list alongside the dial helps explain what is driving the warning.

## Proposed dual filter: evaluated, not adopted

Alongside the 17 September introduction we circulated a proposal: apply the high-dial restriction only when two conditions hold together, a dial of 50 or above **and** SPY less than 2% below its trailing 252-session closing high. That proposal has now been replayed in full, and we are not adopting it.

On SPY returns alone the split still looks suggestive. Within the high-dial group, dates near the high were followed by weaker returns than dates further below it over the next week and month.

| Dial 50 or above | Dates | Next 5 | Next 10 | Next 21 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SPY less than 2% below high | 220 | -0.35% | -0.40% | -1.31% |
| SPY at least 2% below high | 81 | -0.01% | -0.59% | +0.09% |

**Why we rejected it anyway.** Once repeated dates are grouped into episodes rather than counted one by one, the difference between the two halves is not meaningful (t = -0.35). Sorted by how far SPY sat below its high, the restricted group’s average outcome per trade does not move in one direction: -0.28, -0.37, +0.47 and -1.23, in multiples of the risk taken, as you move outward from the high. The 2% cutoff is the flat spot of the sensitivity grid rather than an edge, and at a higher dial threshold the sign reverses.

**Both ways of implementing it finished behind the current rule.** The narrower form, which relaxes the restriction once SPY has already fallen away from its high, gave back a little return and made the worst three-week stretch worse. The broader form, which restricts wherever the two conditions overlap, removed a small group of trades whose average outcome was strongly positive, about +1.25 times risk across nine occasions, and left the deepest drawdown unchanged, so it bought no protection at all.

The proposal was worth testing and the answer was clear. The dial and its 50 threshold stay as they are. Distance from the high remains worth watching, because it is what prompted the idea, but on this evidence it mostly re-labels a distinction the model already makes on other grounds.

The dial readings here combine the production record with the model’s current rules. Since 17 September the published dial is stored directly each session, under the definition dated 18 September, and readings from 2 July 2026 onward are recorded on the day and never revised afterwards. Earlier readings are reconstructed, so they show what the current rules would have produced rather than what was published at the time.
